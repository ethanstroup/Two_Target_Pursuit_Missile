- **Length target: the three prose cells together should run ~600 characters, and must not exceed ~750.** Measured twice now, row length tracks *how the paper was read* rather than how much it matters — rows written straight after a full-text read run 3× the ones written from abstracts, because their author was compressing a finished note instead of summarizing a paper. ## Prose Synthesis

*Cross-cutting observations. Bullets marked ‡ are recent scout/session drafts, closer to what the paper states than to settled synthesis — rewrite or cut them freely.*

**Structural findings**

- **Mutual kill is codimension-one, not an open region — four independent papers, four independent derivations, one answer.** D&S reach it by Rule III set algebra ($M'=\phi$, $M$ collapsing to the four surfaces $|\phi_1|=|\phi_2|$); Merz & Hague geometrically, their collision barrier being a 2D surface in a 3D state space; Getz & Pachter (*JGC*) by their win definition, draws being the points lying *on* the $T_1$ barrier with open regions cleanly assigned either side; Olsder & Breakwell by construction, their seven simultaneous-kill types being literally the pieces $\Sigma$ is built from. **The working assumption for Layer 2 should be that mutual kill is a surface**; an open-volume mutual-kill region would be the surprising, reportable result. D&S's Rule III shows the collapse is parameter-dependent in general, so this is a strong prior, not a theorem. ‡
- **Merz & Hague add a reading D&S does not: the mutual-kill surface is a role-reversal locus** — crossing it swaps who is pursuer and who is evader. If a 2v1 formulation produces a mutual-kill set, look at it as the boundary where role assignment flips rather than as an outcome category. ‡
- **Dimension is not what makes the singular-surface taxonomy explode — two-target structure and asymmetry are.** Merz (1972) takes the two-car problem from second order (homicidal chauffeur) to third and reports that the *only* exceptional surfaces are one universal surface and one dispersal surface, explicitly finding **no new types**. Against that baseline, O&B's 17 local terminal games and M&H's role-reversal surface with dispersal corners are bought by bidirectional lethality and three-way asymmetry, not by state count. Layer 2 adds **target sets**, not dimension, to D&S — so Merz is the wrong end of that lever, and the O&B taxonomy is the better prior for how many pieces a four-target barrier patches from. ‡
- **Kill geometry is bracketed from opposite ends by the two closest structural analogues.** Merz & Hague's condition is purely angular with unbounded range (both kill regions infinite); Olsder & Breakwell's is purely positional — range-bounded, zero angular width. Both are 1v1, bidirectionally lethal, asymmetric, turn-limited games of kind with role determination as the output, differing almost entirely in what "lethal" means. G&P (squint cone + circle), D&S (no-escape envelope) and Dillon (cardioid) sit between them, all range-bounded. Merz & Hague assert — without demonstrating — that their short-range barrier segments are *independent of weapon range*, which if true would let range-free short-range structure port into a WEZ-bounded model. ‡
- **A target-set trade to settle before Layer 2 commits.** G&P's targets are $\theta$-independent cylinders in the reduced space; D&S's are aspect-dependent, keyed to the opponent's angle, and twist with the state. G&P buy a simple terminal manifold and pay in asymmetric dynamics; D&S buy symmetric dynamics and pay with a hard terminal manifold. **Layer 2 inherits D&S's side, so G&P's barrier machinery transfers but G&P's target algebra does not.** ‡
- **Target-set *size* is what breaks a team partition's tidiness — and Pachter et al. say so from the other side of the trade.** Their $R_1/R_2/R_{1,2}$ boundary is a plane only because capture is a point; their own unworked extension notes that giving the pursuers finite capture radii $l_1,l_2>0$ replaces the Apollonius circles by **Cartesian ovals** and makes the partition boundary non-planar. Layer 2 starts where that extension stops — extended targets on all sides — so the one reviewed statement about what target-set geometry does to a *team* partition is a warning that the boundaries will not be tidy. ‡
- **Frame choice is a solution technique, not bookkeeping — a genuinely new axis for this review.** G&P get a *closed* $T_2$ barrier, hence a finite capture region, hence an answer to Isaacs' slower-pursuer problem, only by fixing axes on the **faster player rather than the pursuer**; they say so explicitly and note a pursuer must transform back into his own coordinates. Merz 1972 reaches a closed barrier by a different lever entirely — identity of the players. Pachter et al. add a third variant: axes pegged to the **$P_1P_2$ segment**, i.e. to the *team's* baseline rather than to any one player, which is what makes their whole solution closed-form. Layer 2 has exactly this choice — bandit-fixed, attacker-fixed, or attacker-baseline. ‡
- **D&S's no-escape envelope is a better-justified WEZ boundary than Dillon's cardioid** — derived from an explicit assumption about the target's own optimal evasion, and embedded in a genuine two-sided game rather than a non-reactive OCP.
- **Geometric "safe region" constructs recur under different names** — Yan et al.'s Evasion Space, Mammadov & Ranasinghe's Apollonius disc intersection, Pachter et al.'s BSR.
- **The one reviewed paper that collapses a team into a single player says exactly what that costs.** Hayoun & Shima can pose a two-person zero-sum game only by giving one "player" both pursuer controls, which presumes a single shared objective and no attrition. §5.4's hardest question — is losing one attacker to kill the bandit a win or a trade? — is precisely the thing that collapse assumes away, so the technique is unavailable to Layer 2 for the same reason the question is open. ‡

**Method**

- **Five distinct ways to avoid solving the full value function**, in rough order of generality: (1) Yan et al.'s **KKT/HJI equivalence**, reformulating the PDE itself as a convex program; (2) Weintraub et al.'s **convex-combination-weight trick** for $\mathcal{R}_s$ — choosing $w=-F_2/(F_1-F_2)$ precisely so the Value function's sensitivity to an intractable implicit variable (the aimpoint direction $\phi^*(\mathbf{x})$) cancels identically, never differentiating through $d\phi^*/d\mathbf{x}$; (3) Dillon et al.'s **direct collocation**; (4) **BUP + Lyapunov certification** (G&P) — but this only classifies win/lose/draw and yields no value function; (5) Olsder & Breakwell's **local differential game per terminal configuration**, growing 17 patches from 17 discrete terminal types and assembling survivors by inspection rather than sweeping one barrier from a continuous usable part. (5) is the structural outlier and the natural fallback if D&S's Appendix B rules don't extend to four target sets.
- **A sixth, cheaper trick, from Merz (1972): don't propagate the costates at all.** Merz notes (§1.4) that adjoint discontinuities occur along retrograde paths *before* either switch function changes sign — caused by the evader's dispersal surface — and concludes that strategies can be found "without explicit knowledge of the adjoint vector components." He then builds the whole barrier and the whole game-of-degree map from closed-form state solutions plus matching conditions between competing paths. Worth weighing against D&S's costate/transversality route for Layer 0's close-out, since it sidesteps exactly the numerical fragility that backward costate integration from digitized BUP points invites. *(Outcome, 2026-09-11: the D&S route was taken and the fragility did not materialize — but the reason is instructive. Nothing had to be digitized: all three of D&S's usable-part boundaries and both corner curves are fixed by closed-form conditions, so the BUP is solved rather than read off a figure. Merz's worry applies to a BUP recovered from a plot, not to one recovered from equations — worth checking which case you are in before paying for the adjoint-free route.)* ‡
- **A ready-made acceptance test for Layer 1, and the only one in the reviewed set.** Merz (1972) publishes a *complete game-of-degree feedback solution* on exactly the kinematics D&S — and therefore ProblemStatement §3.1 — inherit, differing only in that his target set is an isotropic $\beta=0.5$ disk rather than a two-sided aspect-dependent WEZ. Running the Layer 1 solver with the disk substituted should reproduce his US and EDS (Figs. 8a–8f) before the same method is pointed at a problem with no published answer. §9 already demands an acceptance test per numerical layer; this is a stronger one than the Layer 0 barrier alone, because it checks *interior* optimal play rather than only the zero level set. ‡
- **A cheap discriminator for classifying dispersal surfaces, also from Merz (1972).** At a dispersal point *on the barrier* the two competing optimal paths have **different** times-to-go; on a game-of-degree dispersal surface they are **equal**, and that equality is literally the equation locating the surface. The two are therefore found by different matching conditions and can be told apart numerically — which will matter as soon as a four-target-set Layer 2 barrier starts producing dispersal structure that has to be sorted into kind-type and degree-type. ‡
- **Carry an invariant the integrator cannot satisfy by accident — and check which of your invariants that is.** D&S's first integral (Eq. 23) is the obvious one to monitor, and it is nearly useless as a test: re-derivation shows the controls **cancel identically** in $d/dt[(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^2/R^2+\lambda_R^2]$, so it is conserved whatever the control does and cannot detect a wrong control law. The semipermeability condition itself, $H^\*=\min_{\sigma_1}\max_{\sigma_2}\lambda\cdot f=0$ (Eq. 15), *can*. In the Layer 0 close-out it exposed two real defects — RK4 stages each re-reading $\operatorname{sign}\lambda$ across a switch, and un-located switch times — while the first integral sat at $10^{-14}$ throughout both. **The general lesson for Layers 1–2: an invariant is only a test of the thing that violates it, so pick the one your likely error actually breaks.** ‡
- **A seventh route, and the one a linearized model buys you: construct the regular zones and take the singular ones as the leftover voids.** Hayoun & Shima propagate candidate optimal trajectories in retro-time from *every* terminal state rather than sweeping a barrier from a usable part, then define each singular zone as a void bounded by same-value trajectories, with the value constant inside. Optimality is certified by showing no two candidates intersect. It is the inverse of the Isaacs construction and is only available because linearization makes the trajectories closed-form — worth knowing as the boundary of what the reviewed methods can do, not as a Layer 2 candidate. ‡
- **D&S's costate/transversality formalism is the missing link** between G&P's geometric barrier-ODE style and Weintraub's HJI-verification style — same object ($\lambda=\nabla V$, $H=\lambda\cdot f=0$), applied to the game of kind instead of the game of degree. **The costate is known only along backward-integrated trajectories seeded from BUP points, never as a global feedback law over winning-zone interiors.** This bullet is the origin of Layer 1: don't expect to simulate "optimal play" from an arbitrary initial condition without further work.
- **The max-min normal-velocity condition $\max_{u_1}\min_{u_2} v_n = 0$ is a reusable primitive** — it finds usable-part boundary points and grows barrier curves from them, and applies to any bidirectional-lethality target geometry including WEZ/cardioid boundaries, not just G&P's circle/squint-cone pairing. *(Now implemented and tested for D&S's three surface families; it also supplies the corner costates directly, see the next bullet.)*
- **A corner condition is cheaper to solve than to transcribe.** D&S spend three pages of closed forms on the multipliers at the target-set corners (Eqs. 38–42, 53–58, 66–70). All of it follows from two conditions the corner costate has to satisfy anyway — $H^\*=0$ and the first integral $=1$ — which reduce to one scalar equation in one angle. Solving that directly reproduces their Eqs. (39)–(41) and (53)–(56) to $10^{-15}$, turning the published closed forms from an input into an independent check. It also surfaces something the closed forms hide: **the corner condition admits more than one admissible root**, the two barrier sheets meeting there, and the paper's formula silently selects one. Layer 2's barrier patches from more corners than this one does, so this is the route to prefer. ‡
- **Direct collocation is the likely fallback for the full problem.** Dillon et al. show indirect/costate methods that solved their single-WEZ case analytically become intractable once a second simultaneous dynamic constraint is added. With two friendlies plus WEZ geometry on both sides, that is concrete evidence for collocation over M&R's greedy law or Yan's closed form once full lookahead is required. (§4 currently recommends a dense HJI grid on three states for Layer 1, with collocation as the fallback.)
- **Published closed forms in this literature do not survive checking — five of the reviewed primaries carry a checkable error in a load-bearing equation.** G&P *JGC* Eq. (10) prints $\sin\theta_0$ where integration gives $\sin\theta$, confirmed against the typeset page, in an equation generating two of four barrier parts; Olsder & Breakwell Eq. (2.8)'s sign, settled against the paper's own Case 5 substitution; **Merz 1972 Eq. (13), which drops a minus sign, contradicting his own Eq. (12) two lines above and his own stated conclusion on the next page**; **Hayoun & Shima Eq. (30), which prints $\mp$ where their own Eq. (27) and Lemma 4.1 both give $\pm$**; and **D&S Eq. (63), which carries one spurious factor of $\operatorname{sign}\phi_1$ in the terminal costate on the off-boresight surface — settled against their own Eq. (64), which the corrected value reproduces and the printed one does not.** Note the pattern in *how* these were caught: **four of the five** were settled by an internal consistency check against another printed equation in the same paper, not by external re-derivation. This is the strongest argument for §9's re-derive-don't-transcribe standard. **The D&S case adds the sharpest lesson about *when* such an error bites:** it is invisible at $\phi_1=+\beta$ and flips the entire barrier strategy pair at $\phi_1=-\beta$, so a check run only on the positive branch — the natural thing to do on a symmetric problem — passes.
- **A fifth defect, of a different and more dangerous kind: a proof that is incomplete rather than a formula that is mistyped.** Hayoun & Shima's Lemma 4.2 asserts two inequalities on the boundary-trajectory slopes of their Eq. (32) and proves them by citing propositions that establish only positivity, not the bound. Both inequalities hold at the paper's own figure parameters and **fail at other parameters satisfying every stated hypothesis** ($\mu_2=12$, or mirrored $\mu_1=12$), so their result looks established for *comparable* strong pursuers rather than all of them. A typo announces itself by contradicting a neighbouring equation; an over-general lemma does not, and is only caught by testing the claim at parameters the authors did not plot. **Worth adding to §9 as a second standard: re-derive the equations, and test the lemmas away from the published figures.** ‡
- **A sixth failure mode, and the only one a reader would never catch by checking algebra: a condition that is necessary but not sufficient, adopted because it looks like a characterization.** Pachter et al.'s Eq. (8) — do the two Apollonius circles intersect? — is *true* as an intersection test and *wrong* as a criterion for cooperative capture, because it also holds on subsets of the solo-capture regions; the correct boundary (Theorem 1's planar wedge) is both different from and **simpler** than the plausible one (elliptical cones). They walk the reader through the false start deliberately. For Layer 2 this is the cheapest available warning: a four-target-set partition will present several natural-looking geometric tests, and passing an algebraic check says nothing about whether a condition *characterizes* the region it was guessed for. ‡
- **A seventh failure mode, and the one that had already reached the codebase: a *parameter* quoted to three significant figures when the paper's own table pins it exactly.** D&S Sec. 4 quotes $\bar R_0=6.14$; their Appendix A Eq. (96) defines it as $(R_{tc})_{\max}/\rho+\pi$, and Table 2's point $A_1$ — which *is* $\bar R(\pi)$ — is printed as 3.0, so $\bar R_0=3+\pi=6.141593$. Four Table 2 entries decide it and 6.14 reproduces none of them. The 0.026% difference had been in the simulation for weeks and moved the long-range outcome fractions by 1.3–1.7 points. **Before adopting a quoted constant, check whether a table of significant points determines it** — and note that this defect class is invisible to every algebraic check, because every equation was right. ‡

**Tactical / problem-specific**

- **Dillon et al. established the WEZ/cardioid framing this research area sits inside**, and are the useful contrast case for what the same Dubins dynamics look like without a second optimizing player.
- **A candidate offensive primitive.** Dillon et al.'s evader shrinks a WEZ's effective range by flying tangent to it, driving bearing toward 90°. The mirror image — one friendly managing aspect to suppress the bandit's effective WEZ while the second closes — is a **direct candidate mechanism for the §5.3 $|\Delta|>2\beta$ result**: one attacker's aspect management is the team's lever on the bandit's single nose.
- **The §5.3 result and Hayoun & Shima's NEZ are the same *shape* of claim, and the difference between them is the point.** Both say a team guarantees an outcome irrespective of the lone agent's play. But the NEZ needs **no coordination** — each pursuer runs the unchanged 1-on-1 law, and the guarantee comes from the mere presence of a second pursuer — whereas *maintaining* $|\Delta|>2\beta$ is coordination in the strict sense. So §5.3 is exactly the kind of result their null hypothesis would not predict, which is an argument for attacking it early: it is the cheapest available test of whether bidirectional lethality makes cooperation pay where one-sided lethality does not. ‡
- **The standing null hypothesis for any claimed team benefit,** from Hayoun & Shima: two strong pursuers each playing the unchanged 1-on-1 strategy. Any Layer 2 cooperation result has to beat two attackers independently running the D&S 1v1 barrier strategy, not merely beat a single attacker. ‡ *(That strategy is now an executable object rather than a description — `barrier.py` / `barrier.js` — so the null hypothesis can be run, not just invoked.)*
- **Pachter et al. bound that null hypothesis from the other side, and the two together say when cooperation pays.** Here the pincer is genuinely cooperative — the aim point depends on both pursuers, and in $R_{1,2}$ neither could do the job alone — while Hayoun & Shima's *strong* pursuers each suffice individually and gain nothing from coordinating. **Cooperation pays exactly where no single teammate is individually sufficient**, which is the hypothesis to carry into §5.3: the $|\Delta|>2\beta$ mechanism should live in the region where neither attacker wins the 1v1. ‡
- **A cheap baseline policy, from Merz & Hague's conclusions:** "turn hard *toward* your opponent" reproduces most barrier maneuvers regardless of role, with exceptions confined to short range where each aircraft is inside the other's minimum-turn circle. Useful as a naive comparison for numerical work, and the location of its exceptions says where the hard geometry lives. ‡
- **Mammadov & Ranasinghe's method is real-time but has no lookahead** — a baseline, not a candidate for game-optimal play.
- **A cheap screening pattern, from Makkapati et al.: test relevance before optimizing.** Their non-degenerate-region construction (an isochrone circle/ellipse bounding where the second pursuer can affect the outcome) answers "does the teammate matter here?" in closed form, before any optimal-control machinery runs. Nothing in the bidirectional-lethality lineage does this explicitly, but it is a cheap, borrowable pattern for a Layer 2 pre-screen — is $A_2$'s presence structurally irrelevant from a given initial state — before the four-target-set machinery is invoked. ‡

## Open Threads / Gaps

*Ordered by relevance to ProblemStatement v0.3.*

### Active — these drive what gets read next

1. **⭐ THE GAP THIS PROJECT SITS IN — bidirectional lethality *and* cooperative team structure.** Every reviewed source with genuine bidirectional lethality (G&P 1981 both papers, D&S 1989, Merz & Hague 1977, Olsder & Breakwell 1974) is **1v1**; every source with cooperative multi-pursuer structure (Weintraub §IV, Yan 2022, Makkapati 2018, Pachter et al. 2019, Hayoun & Shima 2017) has **one-sided lethality**. The intersection is empty. This is §1's defining gap, and it is a **game-of-kind gap — no game of degree required to attack it**. Screen every candidate here first. *(Both sides are now fully documented rather than asserted — D&S and Hayoun & Shima are the cleanest single pair to cite for it, being complementary on every axis and overlapping on none; Pachter et al. 2019 is the cleanest example of the team side solved to closed form, which is what the one-sided assumption buys.)*
2. **D&S's Appendix B synthesis rules extended past two target sets.** No reviewed paper combines that method (decompose into single-target games, correct overlap via $T_i'=T_i\setminus\bar T_j$, recombine by case rules) with **team structure on one side**. §5.5 asks exactly this: does it generalize to **four** target sets when two of them share a single control $\sigma_B$? Either answer is a contribution. Olsder & Breakwell's per-terminal-configuration construction is a second, structurally different candidate technique — weigh it if App. B doesn't extend cleanly.
3. **Nobody has asked what one shared control does to a two-target partition.** §5.2's result — the 2v1 system is two copies of the D&S 1v1 system coupled *only* through $\sigma_B$ — has no analogue anywhere reviewed, because no reviewed source has a lone bidirectionally-lethal agent facing a team. Two sub-questions to hunt: (a) prior work on **angular-separation conditions denying a boresight-limited defender both targets at once** (the $|\Delta|\le2\beta$ one-nose constraint, §5.3); (b) whether the **mutual-kill collapse survives four target sets** (§8.5) — it now has four independent 1v1 sources by four different routes, which argues it is structural rather than a parameter artifact, but the jump from two sets to four is untested. Both answerable at Layer 2.
4. **No reviewed paper gives a global feedback law for the D&S model — this is why Layer 1 exists.** D&S derive $\sigma^*=-\operatorname{sign}(\lambda)$ only along barrier trajectories; interior strategies are arbitrary by definition in a pure game of kind. G&P and Merz & Hague both name the game of degree and decline it; D&S puts it out of scope. So a **1v1 game of degree on a two-target, bidirectionally-lethal, turn-limited model** is itself an unfilled slot — Ardema/Heymann/Rajan's combat-games line, Kelley, and Bera/Makkapati/Kothari are the standing candidates. *(Merz 1972 solves the game of degree on the same kinematics but with a single isotropic target, so he supplies the method and the acceptance test, not the missing result. Pachter et al. 2019 solve one in closed form, but only because simple motion and point capture strip out everything Layer 1 is about.)* **Status 2026-09-11: the "only along barrier trajectories" half is now built and verified** (`Overview/Layer0/Layer0_CloseOut_2026-09-11.md`), which sharpens the thread rather than closing it — the interior remains empty, and that emptiness is now measured rather than argued: perturbing $\bar R_0$ by 0.026% moves the heuristic's long-range outcome fractions non-monotonically by more than a point.
5. **Merz & Hague's own unworked case:** with **bounded weapon ranges on both sides** and the faster aircraft less maneuverable, they claim *both* kill regions may be finite, letting a purely defensive pilot evade indefinitely. Closer to our engagement geometry than the unbounded-range version they solve. Olsder & Breakwell's apparatus formally covers this branch (Sec. 4, cases e–h and $\bar\imath,\bar\jmath$) but their worked example sets $l_2=\infty$ deliberately to switch the escape region off — **so the machinery exists and the case remains undrawn even there.**
6. **The symmetric two-target case — mostly closed, one item genuinely open.** An earlier version of this thread wrongly claimed Olsder & Breakwell and Merz & Hague were the solved symmetric cases; both have since been read and both are asymmetric (M&H in speed and turn rate, O&B in three ways at once), and M&H's draw region depends on that asymmetry. Merz (1972) is the symmetric *kinematic* model but is one-target with fixed roles — **read in full 2026-09-10 and now in the comparison table, which confirms rather than changes that reading.** **What remains open is the unpublished pre-1974 Merz paper Olsder & Breakwell cite in their Sec. 1** — their own two-target role-determination model restricted to identical combatants. That is the closest known prior work to Layer 0, and it may not exist in retrievable form.
7. **Method gap:** no reviewed paper combines **direct orthogonal collocation** with a **true two-sided differential game**. Relevant to Layer 1's method choice.
8. **§5.4's team objective has exactly one reviewed source that argues a cost choice on its merits, and it argues it for the wrong kind of game.** Hayoun & Shima rule out $J_{\min}$ (the outcome depends on one pursuer only, leaving the other with no unique optimal strategy) and $J_{\text{sum}}$ (does not reflect the significance of the minimum) and adopt the product. The reasoning transfers to §5.4; the object does not, since a product of continuous misses is meaningless where the outcome is categorical. **If Layer 1 adopts the signed-time or terminal-margin payoff of §4, a product of the two attackers' margins becomes literally available** — worth deciding deliberately rather than by default. *(Pachter et al. sidestep the choice entirely with a single shared time-to-capture, which is available to them only because the team has one objective and no attrition — the same collapse Hayoun & Shima make.)*
9. **What happens to a team partition when the capture sets acquire size — asked by a reviewed paper, answered by none.** Pachter et al.'s closing paragraph notes that finite capture radii turn the Apollonius circles into **Cartesian ovals** and the planar $R_1/R_2/R_{1,2}$ boundary into "a more complex surface." That is the *degenerate* version of Layer 2's geometry (isotropic disks, one-sided, no turn limit) and it is already unworked, so nothing in the reviewed set says what an extended-target team partition looks like. Cheap to probe numerically at Layer 2, and a natural sanity check before the full four-target construction. *(Nearest existing attempt, from their own reference list: Fuchs, Garcia & Casbeer, NAECON 2018 — 2v1 with a finite capture radius.)*
10. **New, and cheap to settle from a source rather than by re-deriving: the D&S singular-line taxonomy.** Their Table 3 lists 25 singular lines — universal, dispersal, semidispersal, switch, switch-trajectory, equivocal, commuting — and the Layer 0 close-out stops short of locating them, which is what leaves 32 of 59 Table 2 points unplaced and the winning-zone surface unassembled. **One point in that table is not merely unfinished but underdetermined by the printed text:** the paper locates $f_1$ where "$\tilde\lambda_{1f}=0$", but its own Eq. (38) makes $\tilde\lambda_1\equiv0$ along the whole $\phi_2=0$ corner, and the natural reading $\dot{\tilde\lambda}_{1f}=0$ is impossible because Eq. (42) — which re-derives exactly as printed — is strictly signed. **Open marker.** Shinar & Davidovitz's *Unified Approach* (D&S ref. 17, IFAC 1987) and Shinar & Davidovitz's earlier *CMA* 13 paper (ref. 13) are the two places the missing condition would be stated; Lewin (1994) is the standing reference for the taxonomy itself. Worth an acquisition slot only if Layer 2 needs the surface assembled — Layer 1's value function produces the same object globally and more cheaply. ‡
11. **Low priority:** no reviewed paper combines heterogeneous speeds (M&R) with coalition/assignment reduction (Yan). Off v0.3's axis entirely — equal speeds and turn limits are firm at Layers 0–2 — but real if the project relaxes them at Layer 4.

### Deferred — Layer 3 (synchronization), retained not deleted

*§6 defers synchronized impact this semester. These are correct and unresolved, and cannot be closed by a game of kind at all — §6.2 shows impact timing is undefined in the interior of a winning zone — so they are blocked on a 2v1 game of degree regardless.*

- **Three distinct structural relationships to synchronization** appear across the reviewed 2v1 papers, and the taxonomy is what to resume from: **derived from optimal play** (Makkapati 2018; Pachter et al. 2019 — both simple motion, point capture, one-sided); **assumed as a precondition on initial geometry** (Hayoun & Shima 2017); **imposed as a terminal constraint** (Weintraub §IV, via the Apollonius aimpoint). **No source derives it under aspect-dependent bidirectional lethality** — precisely the §6.1 question. *(Pachter et al. and Makkapati et al. are now both read in full and belong firmly in the first category: for Pachter et al., isochrony is an output of the pincer with nothing in the game enforcing it; for Makkapati et al., simultaneous capture is proved by contradiction on the terminal costates — a necessary condition of the evader's optimal play, not an assumption.)*
- **Hayoun & Shima was once the leading "chase this next" bullet; the reading disproved its premise.** $t_{f_1}=t_{f_2}$ is a restriction on admissible initial geometry ($x_1^o/x_2^o=V_{c_1}/V_{c_2}$), not a synchronization mechanism. What survives is non-synchronization: the **product cost** $J=|y_1(t_f)|\cdot|y_2(t_f)|$ (a live §5.4 candidate — now Active thread 8) and the **"cooperation between two strong pursuers is not beneficial"** null result. *(This bullet was silently restored to its retired form once by a stale-baseline overwrite — leave it demoted. The paper is now fully read and in the comparison table; nothing further is owed to it.)*
- **Whether the Apollonius aimpoint construction survives** an adversarial, WEZ-defended target with non-holonomic dynamics is open. Weintraub §IV solves it only for a non-adversarial, non-WEZ, simple-motion evader; it likely breaks the same way Dillon's costates broke once WEZ/Dubins structure entered. *(Pachter et al. give the strongest reviewed statement of how far it does reach — closed-form value function and constant headings — and, in their own extension paragraph, the first hint of how it breaks: finite capture sets replace the circles with Cartesian ovals.)*
- **Candidate transfer technique:** Yan et al.'s KKT/convex-program reformulation may carry a terminal-time equality constraint across two pursuers more directly than a full HJI solve.
